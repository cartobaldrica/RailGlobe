Introduction

Rail Globe presents a world of railroads and stations—nothing else—showing the interconnectedness of one of the most enduring forms of modern transportation. Today, rail is perhaps more important than ever, offering a less carbon-intensive and more efficient form of travel, especially when compared to cars, trucks, and planes. But not all rail is created equal. By reducing the world to its rail, spatial patterns, and inequalities are revealed. These, combined with the stories below, provide examples of how rail relates to our broader social conditions for better or for worse. Rail is important to the future of the planet, but it must be done right.

Explore the map to see how rail is spread across the globe.

Rail Stories

Rail and Labor

Zoom To Spain Zoom To Long Island

People are essential to railroads, and therefore to have good rail, it is necessary to have good people who are well compensated. The widespread practice of devolution and privitization of many national railways is a threat to worker power, and therefore the quality of service a railroad can provide. Over the past few years, rail labor unions have bravely undertaken large actions to improve rail safety and their working conditions. In January, 2026, Spain's largest train driver's union called a three-day strike in response to recent high speed derailments, demanding the implementation of stricter rail safety measures. More recently, five unions representing commuter rail on Long Island, struck to demand wage increases to keep up with the pace of inflation.

An Integrated Timetable

Zoom To

Switzerland is known for having one of the most comprehensive and reliable passenger rail systems in the world. The reliability of Swiss rail is due to the integrated national timetable, which governs across transit modalities. Train schedules are standardized, and also integrated with other forms of transit, such as buses or trams. The timetable is managed by an independent government agency independent of transit providers. Switzerland's railroad success is also enabled by geography: the nation is small, dense, and mountainous, meaning where rail can be built is heavily limited.

Read more about the Swiss integrated timetable: Jokteur

Tren Maya

Zoom To

Categorized as an environmental catastrophe, the construction of the Tren Maya railway represents how railroads can negatively impact local environments and the people that live in them, sacrificed at the interests of global capital. Tren Maya was pitched by the Mexican government in 2018 as a method of drawing more tourists to the area. However, locals righly feared the railroad would open up region to resource extraction and land privitization—threatening Indigenous livelihoods and communal land ownership practices. The volume of tourists projected to use Tren Maya would overwhelm local infrastructure at the expense of the local population. The railroad itself was constructed through portions of a biosphere reserve, prompting the deforestation of over 2 million trees.

Read more about Tren Maya: EJAtlas

Electrification

Zoom To

India's massive rail network is over 99% electrified. The electrification of railways is essential for their decarbonization, and is far more efficient than the use of diesel. India has been electrifying its rail since the early 20th century.

Read more about the electrification of Indian rail: EJAtlas

The United States and Australia both have sparse passenger rail service but are used widely for freight transport.

China has greatly expanded its regional and high-speed rail networks over the past 30 years.

The railway network of Argentina peaked in the 1950s, and since then has been rocked by massive closures and the prioritization of a road transport.

More about this map

What this map shows:

Global passenger rail stops and global railways, which include passenger, freight, narrow-gauge, and disused railways.

These data were collected from OpenStreetMap, a crowd-sourced source of global map data. As such, they are not comprehensive, though for the purposes of the map provide a general portrait of global rail. There are also some oddities and inconsistencies, and that add flavor. For example, peat railroads can be seen in Ireland. More information can be found in the how this map was made section of the site.

What this map does not show:

Currently, the map excludes light rail, streetcars, metro systems, and other forms of local transit. These may be added in the future.

How this map was made

Here I describe step-by-step how this map was made. While I have taken care to make the language accessible, and describe my process in detail, I assume some familiarity with geospatial data and cartography. If you just are intersted in accessing our data, check out the download section.

This map was created using a variety of free and open-source (FOSS) data and technologies, including OpenStreetMap (OSM) and Overpass (the OSM API), OSMPythonTools, QGIS, ProtoMaps, PMTiles, TippeCanoe, and MapLibre.

Rail globe is primarily comprised to two datasets, passenger stops and railways.

Passenger Stop Data

Passenger stops were acquired using a custom Python script built using the OSMPythonTools library. The script goes country-by-country searching for railway stations (railway=station within OSM), and excluding a variety of other station types, such as light rail, tram, monorail, and subway. While these may be added in the future, they were deemed beyond scope for this iteration of the project.

The script creates a .json file with all the passenger stops. This file was then brought into QGIS, and many of the attribute fields were removed to reduce file size. That said, the diversity of languages and information about the passenger stops in the fully dataset is amazing, and there provide useful insights. For the purposes of this project however, a reduction of file size to reduce load-time in the browser was the priority.

Finally, the stop data were converted into a pmtiles file using tippecanoe for seamless viewing in the browser.

Step-by-Step:
    1. Extract data using the stops_scraper.py script.
    2. Remove irrelevant fields from .json.
    3. Convert from .json to .pmtiles using tippecanoe using the following parameters:
    tippecanoe -z11 -Z2 -B6 --projection=EPSG:4326 -o rail_stops.pmtiles -l stops rail_stops.geojson.

Railway Data

Railway data were acquired through ProtoMaps, which aggregates OSM data into a single file that can be queried. Due to the amount of railways across the globe, it was useful to have a straightforward way to make simple queries on a global scale. Unlike the passenger stops, the railway data represent the >railway=rail with no exclusions.

Within protomaps, rail is only visible beginning at zoom 12, and at this level much of the hyperlocal detail is excluded—acceptable for our purpose. Protomaps also uses different attribute tags, with rail identifiable using kind=rail. To begin the process, I generated a protomaps file at zoom level 12, then extracted railways from the file. This produced a .pmtiles file which I then converted into .json and simplified to 5% of nodes. This file was then converted back into a .pmtiles file.

Step-by-Step:
    1. Extract protomaps full-world file with a min-zoom 12 (which is the zoom when railroads appear), using the .pmtiles executable. This Produces a file approx. 10 GB: ./pmtiles extract https://build.protomaps.com/20260422.pmtiles planet_z12.pmtiles —minzoom=12 --maxzoom=12
    2. Filter railways using the tile-join plugin of tippecanoe: tile-join -j '{"*":["all",["==","kind","rail"]]}' -f -o rail.pmtiles planet_z12.pmtiles
    3. Extract a .json file: tippecanoe-decode rail.pmtiles -c > rail.json
    4. Simplify rail.json using mapshaper to 5% using the Douglas-Peucker algorithm. This will make it load much faster.
    5. Delete all but the first 5 attribute fields.
    6. While these data are quite comphrensive, there are some important features missing. Specifically, narrow-gauge railways, and disused railways, which are both important parts of the story. These supplemental data were acquired using overpass-turbo. The query for narrow-gauge rails, with the interface zoomed out to the whole globe, was: [out:json][timeout:500]; nwr["railway"="narrow_gauge"]({{bbox}}); out geom;. For disused railways, the query was: [out:json][timeout:500]; nwr["railway"="disused"]({{bbox}}); out geom;
    7. After generating the data, export each query as a .geojson.
    8. Simplify each .geojson using mapshaper to 5% using the Douglas-Peucker algorithm..
    9. In QGIS, add a field named "kind" for each file. For the narrow_gauge railways, set the value to "ng", for the disused railways, set the value to "du". This will allow these subtypes to be stylized differently than the main rail.
    11. Copy all features from both files into the rail.json file.
    12. Delete unnecessary attributes from the file. We remved everything except the "fid" and "kind" attributes. It is now ready to be converted!
    13. Convert the rail.json file into .pmtiles: tippecanoe -z10 -Z2 --coalesce-densest-as-needed --projection=EPSG:4326 -o rail_routes.pmtiles -l rail rail_routes.geojson
    14. The .pmtiles file is now ready to use!
